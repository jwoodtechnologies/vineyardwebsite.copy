General
What Is a Redevelopment Agency (RDA)?

A Redevelopment Agency (RDA) (also known as a Community Reinvestment Agency, or CRA) is a tool under state law that cities use to turn around neglected or industrial areas to foster investment, improve infrastructure and opportunity where private dollars wouldn’t otherwise go.

In Vineyard, we created the RDA in 2009 to help transform the former Geneva Steel Mill site—a large, contaminated and long-vacant industrial property—into a thriving, livable, and economically sustainable community.

Why Does Vineyard Need an RDA?

Without an RDA, Geneva would not have been transformed. Period.

The land was contaminated, vacant, and unattractive to investors. The RDA made it possible to:

  • Clean up environmental hazards
  • Build roads, water, and sewer systems
  • Support affordable housing and public parks
  • Attract major employers and institutions, such as UVU, Huntsman Cancer Institute, and Top Golf
  • Unlock billions in long-term investment and tax revenue
Without redevelopment, communities like Vineyard are left behind.
How Has the RDA Benefited Vineyard So Far?

Results:

  • Over 20,000 residents now call Vineyard home (up from ~100 in 1989).
  • Thousands of jobs have been created.
  • Major employers and institutions are now investing:
    • Huntsman Cancer Institute (with $22M in land donated by Flagborough Corp.)
    • Utah Valley University Vineyard Campus
    • Top Golf, Megaplex Theaters, Bella’s Market
  • Multifamily housing projects are adding millions in new property tax revenues.
  • Public infrastructure – roads, water lines, utilities – constructed without raising taxes.
All of this was made possible by the RDA. Without it, these investments would never have come.
What Would Not Have Been Built Today Without the RDA?
  • ALL Development North of Center
  • Top Golf and the Megaplex Theater
  • Huntsman Cancer Institute
  • Public infrastructure for roads, sewer, and utilities
  • Parks and community amenities
  • UVU’s campus access and education corridor

These projects required upfront public investment and risk that only an RDA could provide.

Is the RDA Accountable?

Absolutely. Vineyard’s RDA has:

  • Held public meetings and passed all funding through official board votes
  • Commissioned external CPA reviews (K&C CPAs) confirming no fraud or misuse
  • Acted swiftly to address technical accounting issues raised by the State Auditor
  • Launched transparency initiatives to rebuild trust and improve communication
  • No fraud, no mismanagement, no hidden spending
  • All expenditures aligned with adopted project plans and legally binding contracts

Is the RDA Sustainable?

Yes. The RDA is designed to sunset after key goals are met. When that happens:

  • 100% of all property taxes revert to the school district and other taxing entities
  • The area continues to thrive from the permanent infrastructure already in place
  • Vineyard and its partners will see compounded returns for decades
When did Geneva Steel close?
The steel plant closed in 2001 after declaring bankruptcy. The Geneva Steel facility was constructed by the Defense Plant Corporation, an arm of the federal government in 1912. It was purchased in 1946 by USX Corporation (USX), United States Steel Division (USS). Geneva Steel acquired the facility in 1987 and operated the facility until 2001 until declaring bankruptcy. In 2005 Anderson Geneva LLC, and affiliates purchased the land from the Geneva Steel bankruptcy estate and now manage the Site deconstruction, remediation, and redevelopment.  The Bankruptcy trustee sold the steel making equipment to Qingdao Iron and Steel Group Co., Ltd. of China. Qingdao disassembled and removed all of its purchased equipment and completed its work in early 2006. CST Environmental, Inc. purchased the salvage rights from the bankruptcy estate in December 2004 with the requirement to demolish all of the buildings and structures to grade level.
Who is in charge of cleanup of the former steel mill site?
Anderson Geneva is the permitted party responsible for environmental cleanup.
Under federal environmental laws including the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA), the EPA works with state environmental regulators (Utah Department of Environmental Quality, DEQ) to regulate, permit, and oversee environmental remediation and cleanup (“corrective action”) for sites like former steel mills that contain hazardous solid waste. In the case of the former Geneva Steel mill site, a Utah Hazardous Waste Post-Closure Permit was issued to United States Steel Corporation (USS) and the purchaser of the property Anderson Geneva, LLC for post-closure care and facility-wide corrective action for dealing with the waste and debris leftover after closure of the steel mill. This is a highly regulated process which requires complex planning and approvals by the state and federal government and ongoing testing, reporting, tracking, and compliance for a myriad of clean-up activities. This permit was issued in 2004 and updated in 2010. The former mill site has been issued an EPA ID (EPA RCRA ID: UTD009086133) and cleanup progress is closely monitored to comply with all state and federal environmental laws. Cleanup at the site is still ongoing under the permit issued to Anderson Geneva who regularly reports required information to the Utah DEQ.
School
Does the RDA Take Money Away From Schools?

No. This is a critical misconception that must be corrected:

  • Schools keep 100% of the tax dollars they were already getting before the RDA began.
  • Only 75% of new growth dollars are temporarily reinvested.
  • The Alpine School District voluntarily participates in Vineyard’s RDA through a formal Taxing Entity Committee.
  • At the end of the RDA term, schools will permanently receive much more revenue due to increased property values.
Example: Alpine School District has already received substantial new funds from developments like the movie theater and Top Golf, none of which would exist without the RDA.

Finance
Where Does the RDA Money Come From?

The RDA uses a system called Tax Increment Financing (TIF),  which reinvests future property tax revenue growth into redevelopment, not existing public budgets.

The base value of the property is frozen (in Vineyard’s case, extremely low due to the Geneva blight). As new development raises property values, the increase (or “increment”) in tax revenue is reinvested into public improvements in the area.

These funds are not new taxes – they are growth-driven dollars, which would not exist without redevelopment.

Does Tax Increment Financing raise your taxes?
The use of tax increment financing (TIF) in the context of community reinvestment in Utah (RDA, URA, CRA) is not designed with the intent of increasing taxes on individual property taxpayers. Rather, this financing tool re-directs the additional tax revenue from new development for a period of time to use this increased "new growth" revenue to pay for the infrastructure and costs related to redeveloping previously blighted properties. This functions as a deferral of "new growth" revenue from an earlier year to a later year in order to reinvest that new growth revenue to pay for, on a "post-performance" basis, the public costs of redevelopment (like infrastructure).

What this means for the former Geneva steel mill industrial site and the Geneva Urban Renewal Area (Vineyard RDA) is that the vacant and blighted industrial land, after the demolition of the steel mill, is assessed a base value after the demolition. The tax revenue from that base value land continues to go to all local tax entities. The increased value of the land as redevelopment occurs produces new "incremental" tax revenue (essentially "new growth" tax revenue). This additional or incremental tax revenue is then used to pay back the costs of the infrastructure development that facilitated and made possible that increased tax value. A 75% portion of this "incremental revenue" is given to the RDA to pay for these costs after the development has occurred. A 25% share of this incremental revenue still flows to the local government taxing entities. After the increment time period (typically 25 years), all the new tax revenue then goes to the local government entities thus increasing the "new growth" at that time.
 
The reason this tool is designed in this way is that it would be infeasible for a local government to pay for infrastructure to develop blighted or vacant land since that local government doesn't have significant property tax revenue from that land to fund infrastructure. In the case of Vineyard, the vacant industrial steel mill site no longer produced significant property tax revenue for the city such that the city could not raise capital or obtain bonds to build roads or utility systems that makes development possible. However, if the city could immediately finance the creation of roads and utilities, then developers could actually build homes, commercial and industrial developments which would then produce property tax revenue. Tax increment financing makes it possible to do this quickly rather than having that vacant land languish for many years.
